My Story

Background

My career began with a deep interest in the human brain. I've been captivated by questions about how the human brain works - and how intelligence emerges - since I was five years old. That fascination eventually led me to pursue a Ph.D. in neuroscience. Jim Sikela, my Ph.D. mentor, had searched primate genomes for changes in genomic copy number and found something very intriguing: a tiny protein domain whose copy number increased dramatically across primate evolution, reaching its highest levels in humans. Not even an entire protein -- just a small domain. My dissertation was directed at understanding what these domains were doing. Eventually, we discovered something surprising: they appear to slow aspects of development. Humans develop remarkably slowly compared with most of the animal kingdom, a phenomenon known as neoteny. This extended developmental period appears to help make it possible to build an organ as complex as the human brain despite substantial resource and physical constraints.

As a graduate student researcher, I developed skills in computational genomics and bioinformatics, which I ultimately put to work as a faculty member at The George Washington University. As the human copy-number story began to implicate metabolism in the development of a larger brain, I also began working extensively with metagenomic data. Much of this research was conducted using HIVE, a powerful computational platform developed at the FDA to support scientific research and the review of regulatory submissions.

The FDA implementation is an internal system available only to agency personnel, while GW operates a publicly accessible instance through an agreement with the agency. I eventually took responsibility for leading the GW HIVE project. What began as research infrastructure became a much broader platform leadership role.

I worked directly with scientists and regulatory stakeholders to understand their workflows, obstacles, and unmet needs, then translated those needs into user stories, technical requirements, and development priorities. I coordinated with FDA software engineering and DevOps teams to deploy and maintain the platform in highly regulated environments while also leading policy, user support, training, documentation, and usage tracking. That experience taught me that a scientific platform succeeds not only because of what it can technically do, but because users can trust it, understand it, and integrate it into the way they actually work.

That distributed-computing foundation now extends into FEAST, an ARPA-H-funded initiative designed to make sensitive clinical and biomedical data analytically useful without requiring institutions to surrender control of their underlying records. My work has contributed to the platform's product definition, local data environment, interoperability efforts, synthetic development data, access rules, and cross-institutional use-case research.

My work on HIVE also grew into two closely related initiatives. Through BioCompute, I led the development of a data model and JSON schema that translated complex bioinformatics workflows into a standardized, machine-readable representation. Doing that required working across scientific, regulatory, and technical communities to understand their different needs, establish a common set of requirements, and build consensus around a practical solution. I then helped guide BioCompute through IEEE standardization and FDA adoption, directed development of the BioCompute Portal, and led efforts to broaden support across scientific platforms.

Through Argos, I have co-led the development of a regulatory-grade infectious-disease knowledge platform for FDA and BARDA. My role has included translating research, surveillance, and regulatory workflows into requirements for data models, analytical capabilities, quality rules, and visualizations, while coordinating the scientific experts and software developers responsible for delivering them.

Through FEAST, my work expanded further into cross-institutional biomedical data governance. I coordinated GW's participation in an ARPA-H-funded federated-data initiative, bringing investigators, clinicians, IT personnel, data teams, and developers together to define scientific use cases, schemas, access requirements, and institutionally workable approaches to sensitive research data.

These experiences broadened my focus from answering individual scientific questions and managing individual platforms to developing the standards, governance practices, data resources, and institutional collaborations that make entire research communities more capable. Scientific curiosity remains its foundation, but I have found that I am most energized by enabling other scientists: building the platforms, standards, and collaborations that allow entire research communities to do things that would otherwise be impractical. I am especially drawn to the space where science, software engineering, operations, and institutional priorities meet, and to the work required to understand competing needs, establish a shared direction, and turn complex ideas into systems people can actually use.

Big questions are rarely answered all at once. They become answerable by asking better questions. Throughout my career, I've been drawn to problems that are technically complex, scientifically ambiguous, or organizationally difficult to navigate. This website is a collection of those projects, the ideas behind them, and the people and communities that have shaped the journey.

Methods

I enjoy starting with questions that feel too broad, too ambiguous, or too technically challenging to answer directly. Rather than trying to solve them all at once, I try to impose structure: identify the underlying questions, develop practical ways to investigate them, and build solutions that help others explore them as well.

For me, imposing structure begins with listening: understanding what people are trying to accomplish, separating the underlying need from the solution initially proposed, and identifying where different users and stakeholders share common ground. From there, I translate those needs into priorities and concrete work that technical teams can execute, while balancing new capabilities against reliability, policy, security, and obligations to existing users.

Sometimes those solutions are software platforms. Sometimes they're data models, analytical methods, technical standards, or educational programs. The medium changes, but the underlying process does not.

That philosophy has shaped every major project I've worked on, from studying the evolution of the human brain to developing regulatory software platforms, leading international technical standards, and helping scientific communities adopt emerging technologies. The questions and the tools will continue to evolve, but the curiosity remains the same.
